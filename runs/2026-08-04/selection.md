# SELECTION - 2026-08-04 - Carousel No. 25

## THE PICK

**The Dixon Diversion.** FERC's notice on the Alaska Energy Authority's
license amendment application for the Bradley Lake Hydroelectric Project,
docket P-8221-124, published in the Federal Register 2026-08-03, one day
before this run. Public comment and intervention close 2026-08-28 at
5:00 p.m. Eastern.

## THE THESIS

Every deck this series has run about AI infrastructure has been about demand
arriving. Twelve of the last twenty-four topics are some version of a machine
showing up and asking Alaska for ground, gas or megawatts. This deck turns
around and looks at the other side of the ledger, which nobody has drawn.

What does it actually cost Alaska to make more power?

It costs diverting a glacier. AEA proposes to build a new diversion dam that
redirects Dixon Glacier meltwater into Bradley Lake, raise the lake's normal
maximum operating pool by about sixteen feet, and drop a 0.3 MW micro-hydro
turbine into a fishwater release valve. About $400 million. Three years of
study and permitting, then three years of construction. And at the end of it
the plant's installed capacity goes from 119.7 MW to 120.0 MW.

That last number is the deck. Installed capacity moves three tenths of a
megawatt. The energy moves about 38 percent, because the point was never
capacity, it was water. Meanwhile the Department of the Air Force has offered
roughly 4,700 acres across 12 parcels at three Alaska installations for
privately financed AI data centers.

Supply moves in decades and megawatts. Demand arrives in months and, if the
North Slope proposal is to be believed, gigawatts. The asymmetry is the story,
and the comment window is the only part of it a reader can do anything about.

## WHY THIS ONE, AGAINST THE CRITERIA IN ORDER

1. **Concrete Alaska impact.** Bradley Lake is the largest hydroelectric
   project in the state and feeds the Railbelt grid that most Alaskans are
   plugged into. A live federal proceeding decides whether it grows.
2. **Visual potential, and this is where it wins outright.** The story is made
   of drawable things at true scale. A glacier. A lake. A sixteen-foot band on
   a shoreline. A tunnel through rock. The Railbelt spine. The 119.7 to 120.0
   gap, which is a nearly invisible tick that IS the argument, and which the
   artwork can encode as a wordless claim.
3. **Tangibility.** A dollar figure, a date, a docket number, an address to
   file at.
4. **Would an Alaskan send this to a coworker?** A utility person, a
   legislative staffer or a Homer resident sends this the day it posts, because
   it contains a deadline they did not know about.

## THE HONESTY FIREWALL, ARCHITECTURAL AND NOT A FOOTNOTE

Nothing in the record says the Dixon Diversion is for data centers. AEA has
not said it, the FERC notice does not say it, and the deck must never imply
it. This is the single most important editorial constraint on the build, and
it is the same discipline No.24 used when it refused to let a graphite
sentence reach an AI supply chain.

The deck's comparison is between two separately sourced facts about
magnitude, and the slide that makes it says so in its own words. The deck also
never argues that the diversion should or should not be built. It is not an
advocacy deck two weeks before a primary. It reports a proceeding, a
magnitude, and a deadline.

# REVISION AFTER THE CLAIMS GATE (Phase 3, same day)

The fact-checker did its job and the deck changed shape. Recording this in
full, because the version above is now wrong in one load-bearing place.

## WHAT DIED

**The 100 MW per-project minimum is gone.** It was the comparison anchor in
the thesis above, and it does not survive. Zero instances of "100 MW",
"megawatt" or "minimum" in the Air Force's own release or in the solicitation
mirror. Both scouts traced it to a single partisan outlet. It must not be
printed. With it went the reported $500 million minimum, the up-to-50-year
lease term, the Stak Energy 1 to 3 gigawatt fallback anchor, and the
eight-data-centers-in-Alaska inventory. Also dead is the Enstar framing about
evaluating whether it is prudent to take new customers, which appears in
neither supplied source.

Nine drops in total. The deck lost every load-side megawatt figure it had.

## WHY THAT MAKES THE DECK BETTER

The original deck compared 0.3 MW to 100 MW. That deck no longer exists,
and the deck that replaces it is truer.

What the Air Force actually published is acres. Approximately 4,700 of them,
across twelve parcels, at Joint Base Elmendorf-Richardson, Eielson and Clear,
and a Deputy Assistant Secretary saying on the record that the offer supports
"the growing demands of the AI industry". The release names acres. It names
parcels. It names a named official. It names no megawatts, and neither does
the solicitation record. There is no verified public number for how much
electricity any of it would draw.

Set that against the other side of the ledger, where the numbers are almost
comically precise. To add about 38 percent more energy at Alaska's largest
hydro project, AEA proposes to divert the meltwater and surface runoff of a
glacier, raise a lake about sixteen feet, and run it through what Petroleum
News reports is a 4.6 mile tunnel. About $400 million. Three years of
permitting, then three of construction. And the installed capacity goes from
119.7 MW to 120.0 MW, because the point was never turbines, it was water.

## THE THESIS, REWRITTEN

**One side of Alaska's power ledger is measured to the tenth of a megawatt.
The other is measured in acres.**

Alaska is being asked to supply a demand for which nobody has published a
number. The supply side is documented down to a fishwater release valve. The
demand side is documented in land. And the only number on either side with a
deadline attached to it is a FERC docket that closes August 28 at 5:00 p.m.
Eastern, which is 1:00 p.m. in Alaska.

The asymmetry of UNITS is the argument, and it is a gift to the artwork. The
deck does not have to assert a comparison it cannot prove. It has to draw two
ledgers that do not share a unit, and let the reader see that they do not.

## THE HONESTY FIREWALL, NOW ARCHITECTURAL RATHER THAN A CAVEAT

The fact-checker's first warning is the design brief for an entire slide.
Nothing in any fetched document links the Dixon Diversion to data centers.
AEA's pages, the FERC notice and Petroleum News mention neither AI nor data
centers even once. These are two independent records with no shared actor,
document or docket, and the danger is pure adjacency. Put C08 next to C20
with no separating language and the reader concludes the glacier is being
diverted to feed a data center.

So the deck states it outright, at full weight, on its own slide, in the same
type size as the argument. AEA has not said this project is for data centers.
It is not being built for them. That nobody has published the number that
would connect the two sides IS the finding, not a hedge against it.

Two more constraints from the gate, both binding on copy:
- C07 (0.3 MW) may never appear without C08 (38 percent) on the same slide.
  Capacity and energy are both true and a slide carrying only one of them
  argues by omission. This is the deck's most likely factual error.
- C26, roughly 149,000 additional megawatt hours a year, is OUR arithmetic
  and nobody else's. It carries that label on the slide and in the caption.

## WHAT THE DECK NO LONGER DOES

It does not say a data center would consume the whole expansion. It does not
rank the two sides. It does not argue the diversion should or should not be
built, two weeks before a primary. It reports a proceeding, two magnitudes
that do not share a unit, and a deadline.
