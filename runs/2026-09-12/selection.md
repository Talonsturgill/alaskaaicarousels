# SELECTION — No.57 — 2026-09-12

## THE DECISION

**NMFS has issued the Office of Naval Research a permit to make noise in the
Beaufort and Chukchi, and what the noise is for is telling robots where they are
under the ice.**

Published September 10th as document 2026-18459. The action line reads "Notice;
issuance of incidental harassment authorization", so this is a decision and not a
proposal. It runs from September 14th, 2026 to September 13th, 2027, and the first
cruise leaves this month.

The activity is Year 9 of the Arctic Mobile Observing System. GPS does not
penetrate sea ice, so an autonomous vehicle under it has no way to know where it
is. The answer being built in Alaska's water is acoustic, six moored sources on
the seafloor and two buoys drifting in the ice, and a vehicle works out its
position by listening, the way a receiver works out a position from satellites.
The signal is sound, and sound in that water is also how beluga find each other
and how a hunter on the ice finds a beluga.

## WHY THIS ONE, against the four criteria in order

**1. Strongest concrete Alaska impact.** 639,237 square kilometres of the Beaufort
and eastern Chukchi inside the US exclusive economic zone, coming within 204
kilometres of the Alaska coast. The species the authorization is written about are
beluga and Arctic ringed seal, which are subsistence species for North Slope and
Northwest Arctic communities, not abstractions. The ship is R/V Sikuliaq, which
Alaskans crew. The impact is not a forecast, it is a permit with an effective date
four days from now.

**2. Visual potential, and this is where it beats every other candidate
outright.** The story's geometry is a water column. Ice on top, six moorings
standing on the seafloor, two buoys adrift above, a REMUS 600 and a fleet of
gliders in between, and the whole thing works by range circles from fixed points
intersecting, which is a diagram that draws itself and encodes the argument at the
same time. There is a real quantity at every scale, 639,237 square kilometres, 204
kilometres of standoff, six sources, a 30-second pulse every four hours, 180 dB.
And it is the first story in a month whose art is a PLACE rather than a document.

**3. Tangibility.** A reader can hold the whole mechanism in one sentence. Sound
instead of satellites.

**4. Would an Alaskan send this to a coworker?** Yes, and to two different
coworkers for two different reasons, which is the test passing twice. Anyone who
works on the Slope sends it because under-ice autonomy is the enabling layer for
everything anyone plans to do up there. Anyone who works in co-management sends it
because the only public document in the whole program is a finding about whether
the noise will injure a seal.

## THE THESIS THE DECK ARGUES

**REWRITTEN AFTER THE CLAIMS GATE, and the rewrite is the fact-checker earning its
place.** The thesis this document carried an hour ago was that the permit licenses
noise in water beluga, ringed seal and Iñupiat hunters share. The first two are
verified. The third is not in the record at all. Both notices were searched for
Iñupiat, Alaska Native, tribal, hunt, hunter, harvest and co-management, and the
only hit in either document is the statutory test recited at C48, carrying its own
qualifier, "(where relevant)". The study area's closest approach to the Alaska
coast is 204 kilometres. So that framing is REMOVED rather than softened, and what
replaced it is stronger, because it is what the documents actually say.

**THE PERMIT KNOWS THE MACHINE'S POSITION AND DOES NOT KNOW THE ANIMAL'S NUMBER.**

The whole point of the thing being authorized is precision. Under ice, the notice
says, gliders navigate by trilateration from moored acoustic sound sources, the
geometry of intersecting circles, or else they dead reckon (C25). Six fixed sources
and two drifting ones, transmitting for twelve months, so a vehicle can answer the
question where am I.

Now read the same document's stock table. The Beaufort Sea beluga abundance
governing a 2026 permit is **39,258, from a 1992 survey** (C41). Thirty-four years
old. The Eastern Chukchi figure is 13,305 from 2017 at a coefficient of variation
of 0.51, which is imprecise on its face (C42). And the Arctic ringed seal, the one
species in this authorization listed as threatened under the Endangered Species Act
(C39), has a stock abundance the table gives as **UND**, undetermined, with its
potential biological removal also undetermined (C43).

That is the argument, and it is not a complaint about the Navy. It is a structural
observation with a document behind every clause. The instrument gets a position fix
to the metre. The threatened animal it is permitted to disturb has no published
number at all. And the finding that the disturbance is acceptable rests on a
Biological Opinion issued on September 13th, 2022 (C40), inside an instrument that
by statute can't run longer than one year (C47), now in its ninth (C06), on a
public record consisting of three comment letters from private citizens (C44).

The house voice says that plainly. Not that the grid should not exist, but that
nine years of building one has produced better information about where a robot is
than about how many seals are in the water with it.
