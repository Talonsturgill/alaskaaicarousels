# SELECTION — 2026-07-26 — Carousel No. 18

## THE DECISION

**Gov. Dunleavy signed a promise about who pays for AI power. In Alaska,
the only utility that signed it is a 1,566 customer cooperative with no
transmission line out of town.**

The story is the gap between a promise and an instrument. On July 23, 2026
the White House expanded its Ratepayer Protection Pledge and 23 Republican
governors signed an addendum committing to implement its principles "to
the greatest extent possible" (C01, C02). The pledge itself binds signing
COMPANIES to five concrete things, starting with build, bring or buy new
power supply (C06). Both USA TODAY and POWER describe it as voluntary and
non binding (C03, C04).

Read live on July 26, the signatory list carried 281 names (C10). Exactly
one is an Alaska entity, Cordova Electric Cooperative (C11, C12, C13),
which serves 1,566 customers, has 18 MW of total generation, is not on the
state highway system and is not connected by transmission line to any
other community (C14, C15, C16). Cordova also already hosts a data
center, at 150 kilowatts, on hydro (C17, C18). NOTE, the superlative
"Alaska's working data center" was killed by the copywriter because the
Alaska data center count failed verification, and it appears on no slide.

Meanwhile SB 250, which would have made the same cost protection legally
binding through commission approved contracts, passed the Alaska Senate on
May 16 and died in House Community and Regional Affairs (C19, C20, C21).
SB 2, which would have governed state agency AI use and election deepfake
disclosure, died after a single hearing in April 2025 (C22). Alaska's US
House member has a bill pointing the other way entirely, letting large
loads run on a self contained grid of one, outside the shared system
(C24, C25). And 4,700 acres across twelve parcels sit offered at JBER,
Eielson and Clear for AI data centers (C26, C27) against a Railbelt
reported in March 2026 to be able to absorb about 25 MW (C29).

### Why it wins on the four criteria

1. **Concrete Alaska impact.** The dominant Alaskan objection to AI
   projects this summer, per the community scout, is "this will raise my
   power bill." A sitting governor just made a public commitment about
   exactly that, eight days after the state's most watched comment window
   closed. Whether that commitment can bind anything is a question every
   candidate on the August ballot can be asked.
2. **Visual potential.** The story's central fact is geometric and
   drawable. A transmission line either continues or it stops. One
   signature on a list of 281. Five commitments that exist as text and
   nowhere in Alaska statute. 150 kilowatts against 4,700 acres.
3. **Tangibility.** Named decider, dated act, primary sources, a real
   town, a real cooperative with published numbers.
4. **Would an Alaskan send this to a coworker.** The Cordova detail is
   the kind of fact people repeat. It is funny and sad and completely
   verifiable.
