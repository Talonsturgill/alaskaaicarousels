## THE CANDIDATE FIELD, ranked

### 1. ANCHORAGE'S POLICE AI ORDINANCE (beats E and F, independently)

**Two scouts on different beats surfaced this as their top finding with matching
numbers, which is the strongest corroboration signal this process produces.**

The Anchorage Assembly holds a public hearing on **September 1st, 2026** on a
companion pair. One is roughly a $600,000 three-year equipment contract with
ThunderCat Technology, paid from federal and state fine and forfeiture funds, to
build out the Anchorage Police Department's Real-Time Crime Center on Elmore
Road. The other is an ordinance by Keith McCormick, Daniel Volland and Sydney
Scout that would write rules for the thing before it is finished.

The system is designed to stream **up to 750 camera feeds**. It launched in
September 2025 with **about 15 cameras and one employee**. More than 250 city
traffic cameras could join it, plus roughly **216 private cameras** residents
registered voluntarily, plus drone and body-worn footage and automatic plate
readers. The Assembly has already appropriated **$11.8 million**.

The ordinance's four safeguards, in the co-sponsor's own words, are a case or
incident number and specific justification for every search and every live
monitoring session, automatic deletion of surveillance data after two weeks
unless lawfully preserved for an active case, plate readers restricted to
specific public safety investigations and barred from parking and code
enforcement, and Assembly approval before adding new surveillance capabilities,
"including AI-powered tracking or identification technology."

**AN ACCURACY TRAP, flagged for the fact-checker.** The three sources describe
the AI provision differently. The sponsor's op-ed says Assembly approval is
required "before adding new surveillance capabilities, including AI-powered
tracking or identification technology." Alaska Public Media reports it as the
crime center's use of AI needing Assembly approval and states the ordinance
carries no specific facial-recognition or human-feature-recognition provision.
ADN's account is the one that mentions human-feature recognition. **The
defensible on-slide wording is the sponsor's own.** Do not print
"human-feature recognition" as the ordinance's text. Anchorage separately banned
facial recognition in 2023 and Chief Sean Case says that ban stands and that APD
uses object recognition only, for plate reading, having never tested face
recognition.

Current retention, for contrast with the proposed two weeks, is 14 days for
plate records and a 3 to 5 day overwrite for traffic camera footage, absent an
evidentiary hold.

**Why it wins.** Concrete Alaska impact, since Anchorage is roughly 40 percent of
the state. A reader-actionable date seven days out. Numbers that carry an
argument by themselves. And it is the rare AI story where the decision is still
open rather than already made.

**Verified directly by the showrunner** against all three sources, not taken on
the scouts' word. Ordinance number is not stated in any of them.

### 2. ALASKA'S HYDRO FLEET AGAINST ONE CAMPUS ASK (beat A)

Governor Dunleavy's August 24th Alaska Hydropower Day proclamation, a primary
document published yesterday, puts Alaska's whole hydroelectric fleet at **more
than 50 facilities and over 480 megawatts**, 29 percent of 2025 electricity and
90 percent of renewables. The STAK Energy Campus proposed on 715.4 acres south
of Deadhorse is described in the state's own lease documents as needing **1 to 3
gigawatts** of on-site gas generation. Strong arithmetic. Held back because STAK
was the 2026-08-06 deck and the proclamation is the only genuinely new element.
