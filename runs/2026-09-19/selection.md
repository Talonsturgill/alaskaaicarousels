# SELECTION — 2026-09-19 — Carousel No. 63

## THE STORY

**Indigenous observations from the Alaska Arctic coast exposed an error in the
ocean model federal managers intend to forecast with, and the correction was
published on September 18th.**

Claudine Hauri and Donna Hauser of UAF's International Arctic Research Center,
with Inupiaq observers including Billy Adams, Roberta Glenn and Joseph Leavitt as
named co-authors, published "Indigenous Knowledge and observations improve Arctic
Ocean model skill and relevance" in Communications Earth and Environment on
September 18th (DOI 10.1038/s43247-026-03899-w).

The team evaluated the MOM6-COBALT ARC11k coupled ocean, sea ice and
biogeochemistry hindcast for 1993 to 2023 against more than 12,000 records from
the Alaska Arctic Observatory and Knowledge Hub, an observer network running
since 2006 along more than 1,500 km of coastal Arctic Alaska. During the 2019
marine heatwave the model put freeze-up at Utqiagvik earlier than observers
actually recorded it. The paper's argument is that conventional scientific
validation data would not have surfaced that error, and it offers an "interwoven
seasonal calendar" as a transferable evaluation method. The modeling sits under
NOAA's Changing Ecosystems and Fisheries Initiative.

## WHY THIS ONE

Against the Phase 4 criteria, in order:

1. **Concrete Alaska impact.** CEFI models are the forecasting layer federal
   managers intend to lean on for Bering and Arctic fisheries and ice conditions.
   A documented, peer-reviewed method for catching their blind spots is
   load-bearing for North Slope and Bering communities, and for anyone whose
   season opens on a model's number.
2. **Visual potential.** Very high and unusually specific. A 1,500 km ribbon of
   coast carrying 12,000 observation ticks. A seasonal calendar as two concentric
   rings, model on the inside and observation on the outside, with 2019's
   freeze-up showing as a visible arc of disagreement. An 11 km grid laid over a
   coastline to show what a grid cell does and does not resolve.
3. **Tangibility.** Freeze-up is the most tangible date in northern Alaska. The
   error is a date being wrong, which needs no translation.
4. **Would an Alaskan send this to a coworker?** Yes, and to a different coworker
   than the data-center decks reach. It is the rare AI story where Alaskans are
   the ones correcting the machine rather than absorbing it.

It also changes register. Eight of the last twelve decks have been data centers,
federal orders and procurement. This is a research story with named Alaskans in
the author list.
