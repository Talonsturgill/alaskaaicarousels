# SELECTION — carousel No. 33, 2026-08-14

## THE STORY

**NSF opened federal money for AI compute infrastructure on August 4th, 2026,
and wrote a rule that caps every state at one award.**

Solicitation NSF 26-513, State and Regional Artificial Intelligence
Infrastructure Hubs, posted July 31st and announced August 4th. NSF announced
it as a $100 million initiative (C10) and the solicitation puts anticipated
funding at $40 million to $100 million (C11). It anticipates 10 awards in each
cycle (C12) and expects proposals to request $4 million to $12 million over 5
years (C13). Only one award per state or multi-state region (C01). An
institution may appear as a participant in at most one proposal, and if it
appears on more, only the first is reviewed (C03, C04). Full proposals are due
November 4, 2026 (C06).

NSF's stated reason for the program is that access to AI infrastructure remains
highly uneven across the nation (C08, C09).

## THE THESIS

The remedy is distributed at a flat rate, and the problem it names is not flat.
One award per state treats Alaska and Rhode Island as the same size of need,
though Alaska's land area is about 552 times Rhode Island's (C23, C24). So
Alaska's whole claim on the program is one application, and the only date that
matters is November 4th.

NOTE, corrected after the fact-checker's pass and again after the art
prototype. Two earlier versions of this thesis were wrong and both are recorded
below rather than quietly overwritten. The solicitation does NOT forbid Alaska
from spending its own money on compute, it bars a proposer from pledging
voluntary committed cost sharing inside the proposal, which is routine NSF
boilerplate (C05). And one award per state is a CEILING and not a floor, so the
argument is not that Alaska's allocation is too small, it is that Alaska's
ceiling was set before any proposal was read.

This is a claim about a RULE, not a complaint about a grant, and it is provable
from the solicitation text alone. That is what makes it shippable on a day when
the search budget is gone and only fetchable primary documents remain.

## WHY THIS, AGAINST THE CRITERIA

1. **Concrete Alaska impact.** The cap binds Alaska absolutely and the deadline
   has not passed. A reader at the University of Alaska, at Alaska Pacific, at
   Ilisagvik, or in a legislative office can act on this. Contrast every other
   in-window candidate, which reported a decision already made.
2. **Visual potential.** The story's geometry is the reason it beat the field.
   The rule pays no attention to area, population or existing capacity, so the
   art can draw the thing the rule refuses to see. Alaska is the largest state
   and its allocation is identical to the smallest. True lon/lat Alaska geodata
   and the places gazetteer are already in assets/, and the deck's central
   image is cartographic rather than diagrammatic, which is what this run needs
   given the standing weakness below.
3. **Tangibility.** Dollars, a count of awards, a count of states, a date.
4. **Would an Alaskan send this to a coworker?** Yes, and to a specific
   coworker, which is the better test. This is a forwardable document for
   anyone at a university, a tribal college or a legislative office.

## EDITORIAL NOTES CARRIED FORWARD

  Nothing in the record says any of that. What the record says is that the
  window is open and the cap is written.
- The deck must not treat one award per state as a scandal. It is a defensible

  which is a structural observation and survives being fair to NSF.

  runs against NSF's own stated goal using NSF's own words. That is the
  strongest and most honest form available.

## A PRECISION POINT THE ART CAUGHT, 2026-08-14

Prototyping the 50-state image surfaced an error that would have shipped as a
wrong argument, so it is recorded here before the storyboard is written.

"One award per state" is a CEILING, not a floor. The solicitation anticipates
10 awards in each cycle (C12) and there are 50 states, so at most 10 states get
a hub in the first cycle and no state can get two. The first version of this
deck's thesis quietly assumed every state receives one and that Alaska's
grievance was the SIZE of its allocation. That is not what the document says.

The accurate and stronger argument is this. Alaska is capped at one and is
competing for one of ten, and the cap was set before any proposal was read.
The prototype that drew an identical gold token under all 50 states was
therefore drawing a false picture, and the shipped version must show that most
of those states will end the cycle with nothing. Ten tokens, fifty states, and
the largest state's ceiling identical to the smallest's.

## THE DIRECTORS ROOM, AND THE SYNTHESIS

Three lenses, three complete treatments, and the room was unusually good. All
three are preserved in the run record because two of them contributed organs to
the deck that shipped.

### The pitches

**CARTOGRAPHER, THE EQUAL-AWARD PROJECTION.** The deck is one chart sheet and
the thing that changes slide to slide is the PROJECTION. Every honest map
prints a note declaring what it preserves, so this one does too, and on the
slides that draw NSF's rule the note has to admit the rule's projection
preserves the state count and gives up area. A two-plate print, land engraved
in white-line intaglio, the rule printed over it in chart magenta as a
regulated-area overprint. A magenta ring of constant 48 px diameter on every
slide, never scaled, whose meaning comes entirely from what is underneath it.

**DATA JOURNALIST, THE SET MARK.** A brass survey cap of constant physical
diameter set into a modelled ground whose extent is the variable. Sharpest
thesis language of the three, that this is a DENOMINATOR story, NSF picked a
denominator (states, n equals 1 each) and the problem it named lives in a
different one. Best claim-to-parameter discipline, including 82 chain ticks
across the deck as the countdown.

**HISTORIAN OF THE FUTURE, THE CAST SENTENCE.** The eleven word rule as an
issued cast aluminium marker plate, identical to every unit of ground in the
country, photographed on ground that is not identical. Best prose, and the best
cover mechanic, promise the sentence and hand it over on slide 2. Its continuity
device made the sun's elevation a linear map of the countdown.

### The decision, and the reasoning

**THE CARTOGRAPHER WINS THE STRUCTURE.** Two of its ideas are better than
anything else in the room, and both attack this run's declared weakness rather
than decorating around it.

The first is the LOWER NEATLINE MARGIN. A chart's marginalia (projection note,
scale bar, source line, counter) lives at the bottom of the sheet, is required
by the form, and is information rather than decoration. Combined with a key
lamp at the NEAR edge of the table, at azimuth 118 and elevation 26, the
brightest modelled tone in every frame falls in the lower third by
construction. The dead lower zone has been named by the scorer in six
consecutive runs and answered each time with a note. This answers it with a
form that cannot be built without filling that band.

The second is the CONSTANT RING. A 48 px magenta ring on all nine slides,
never scaled once, whose meaning is entirely supplied by what sits under it.
That is the rule itself drawn as an object, and it is the cleanest wordless
encoding of C01 available. On the ranked strip the rings at the small end
physically overlap, because those states are smaller than their own awards,
and that is a computed consequence rather than an effect.

**ORGANS TAKEN FROM THE OTHERS.**
- The data journalist's DENOMINATOR framing is the sharpest statement of the
  thesis in the room and it goes into the copy brief verbatim.
- Its 82 tick countdown and the cartographer's 82 tick neatline converged
  independently on the same device, which is usually a sign the material wants
  it. It ships on S8.
- The historian's FAIRNESS BEAT and the cartographer's S7 also converged. The
  deck argues NSF's own case, properly, before it disagrees with it.

**TWO CHANGES MADE AGAINST THE WINNING PITCH.**

1. **The deck is INVERTED to a night plate.** The cartographer pitched a
   mid-value buff sheet, flagged the light-register risk itself, and offered
   the inversion as a cheap escape hatch. Taking it, because the budget is
   spent. CADENCE says one light deck per 8 runs and No. 30 on August 9th was
   the first light-register deck in at least nine runs, which is four runs ago.
   White-line intaglio is by definition a white-on-dark technique, so this is a
   two token palette change and not a redesign.
2. **Slide 4's isobaths are cut.** The cartographer's own self-critique got
   there first, that soundings are one translation too many on the deck's most
   numerically delicate beat, where a reader has to hold two dollar figures at
   once. Replaced with the data journalist's version, a cabinet oblique casting
   with two scribed lines at $40 million and $100 million and the volume
   between them deliberately left unfilled, which is honest about a range being
   a range and is still a modelled object rather than a bar.

**WHAT WAS NOT TAKEN, and why.** The historian's SINKING KEY, a sun elevation
that falls linearly with the countdown, is the most beautiful single device
pitched. It is incompatible with a chart lamp clamped to a table, and the deck
can only have one light. Recorded here because it deserves to be tried on a
future deck with an exterior register.

The historian also caught its own trap, that one object across nine slides is
the exact failure this run is attacking, and its proposed fix was to cut the
plate from the map slides and let the land carry them. The cartographer's
structure already does that, which is a third piece of independent convergence
and is most of why it won.
