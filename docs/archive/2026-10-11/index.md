# Who Sets the Clock on a Plate Read

> In the Anchorage mayor's version of the surveillance ordinance, a plate data "limit" is a reason, not a number.

Published 2026-10-11 by Alaska AI. 9 slides.
Canonical: https://alaskaaihq.com/archive/2026-10-11/

## The story

14 days in code, or a limit set in policy?

Three texts. One sets a day.

The sponsors' August 18th original would delete plate data within 14 days, and searching older data would need a warrant. The mayor's September 15th substitute asks for a policy limit "based on articulable operational needs." The sponsors' own S-1 builds on the mayor's text.

The original timed the live feed too.

Live camera access would shut off after sixty minutes, renewed thirty at a time. Trials of new tools, 90 days at most. Plate reader authority, ending January 1st, 2031. The September texts set other clocks, 30 days to report a policy change and 45 to act on a complaint.

September's texts name artificial intelligence.

Both define surveillance technology as tools that work "by using fully or partially-automated machine learning, artificial intelligence, or similar technology." The August original defined it by what a system collects.

There is already a 14.

APD's own page says scanned plate data is deleted after 14 days. That's police policy on a web page, not code.

Three texts, side by side.

No version reaches this pole.

Flock says it began installing plate readers for a private customer in Alaska the week before October 5th. A crowdsourced map showed eight at two Anchorage Lowe's stores, ADN reports. APD says it doesn't use Flock and has no access to them. Each text governs city agencies.

October 20th could set the city's clock.

The Assembly returns to AO 2026-108 on October 20th, after a work session scheduled for October 14th. The crime center it governs could connect up to 750 cameras. In late August, 238 were registered with APD and 11 integrated, per KTUU.

Where should Anchorage write its number, in code or in policy?

Say which below.
