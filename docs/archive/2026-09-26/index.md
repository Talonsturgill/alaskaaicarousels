# Who Read It? What 90 Alaska News Labels Said

> Alaska News says its old footer told every reader an article was "reviewed by editors," whether or not it was.

Published 2026-09-26 by Alaska AI. 9 slides.
Canonical: https://alaskaaihq.com/archive/2026-09-26/

## The story

"Source access is not the same thing as editorial judgment."

90 articles in 48 hours. One human name on the review line.

Alaska News says every article carries a line saying whether a person reviewed it. Every human name on those lines was the editor-in-chief's, Cale Green.

The machine already does the reading.

Alaska News says its tool has sat through 1,258 recorded public meetings and 3,092 hours of audio. A team of five based in Anchorage runs it. How would a reader know who read each draft?

The right invention is the label.

When an editor has read the whole piece, the article says reviewed by, with a name. When no one has, it says that instead. Its old footer told every reader "reviewed by editors," regardless.

What the labels on 90 articles said.

Counted from the alaskanews.com news sitemap, September 26th. A label describes process, not accuracy, and can change after a later review.

Plenty of reading. One name on the record.

The risk it names itself.

Alaska News says some articles publish without a person reading them first. Its story on the Anchorage Assembly's Axon police-data memo ran as "AI-assisted. No editor review is on record for this article." That doesn't make it wrong. It tells you to check.

"The strongest protection is not my assurance. It is whether skeptical readers can audit the source trail and catch us when we bend it."

Alaska News says the source wins when a summary and its record disagree. Readers can only audit what the label tells them.

Should every AI-drafted story in Alaska say who reviewed it, by name?
