Alaska AI

Field study prepared for Anchorage Neighborhood Health Center

You already put AI into clinical production. Your front door still ends in a callback.

Anchorage, AlaskaAugust 6th 2026

In short

You have done the hard part already. An ambient AI tool drafts your clinicians' visit notes and your Chief Medical Officer talks about it publicly. Nothing like it appears on the pages we checked at Mat-Su, Interior and Southcentral Foundation.

So we looked at your front door. Someone who registers online waits, in your own words, "Within 1-2 business days" for a text with the intake form links. Then, again in your words, "Within 1-2 business days" for a call to schedule. Anyone not seen by an ANHC provider in 36 months re-enters that loop. On the same afternoon, a Providence clinic on Huffman shows new patients open appointment times on screen, which is not a switch you alone could throw, because your portal link resolves to an Epic MyChart login run for Providence.

Here is what we could not find, and it turned out to be the finding. Nobody publishes how long that loop takes, how many people fall out of it, or what it costs, and from outside there is no way to tell whether it is tracked internally. A published process with an unmeasured cost is one you can't defend to a board.

What we would build is mostly not AI. When we scored the options the version with no model in it beat the version with one, so phase one is timestamps plus an automatic send and contains no model at all.

The ask is 9,500 dollars, one fixed fee for the organization, never per clinician and never per seat. On recovered staff time alone the conservative case gives back about 35 percent of what this costs you over five years, fee plus your own hours plus running it plus a contingency we added to our own side, and never pays back. We lead with that rather than bury it. What the money buys is three measurements you do not have, a permanent fix to the first published wait, and a written yes or no on the larger model work decided on your numbers rather than ours, for roughly a fifth of what one of your posted front office roles costs in a year.

What we could not verify is listed at the end, and one thing matters most. Your pages say the intake links arrive within one to two business days and never say why. If somebody is checking identity or looking for a duplicate chart in that window, an instant send would be the wrong build, and we would rather learn that in week one from your staff than assume our way past it.

01

Your own pages describe a front door that always ends in a callback

Two published waits, and a competitor across town that books on screen.

Your new patient page sets out the sequence plainly. After someone registers, "Within 1-2 business days, you will receive a text with links to complete the following forms." After the forms, "Within 1-2 business days, you will receive a call to schedule your first appointment." Each is published as a range rather than a moment, and the page does not say what happens inside it. The population hitting that loop refreshes continuously, because your own definition counts anyone not seen by an ANHC provider in the last 36 months as new.

We are describing your published service level, not a failure. A one to two business day promise is a real commitment and plenty of clinics publish nothing at all. The point is narrower. First contact does not produce an appointment, and what happens inside those windows nobody outside can see.

1-2 business days

Twice, in your own words, on your own new patient page. Once for the forms and once for the scheduling call.

For contrast on the same day in the same city, Providence's Huffman page offers new patients open appointment times on screen. We are not holding that up as a standard you failed. Providence is a large system with a different balance sheet, and it is simply the alternative an Anchorage patient has in front of them at the moment they choose.

02

The real cost is that nobody can say what the loop costs, including us

Three numbers that decide this, and none of them published anywhere.

We went looking for how long your intake loop takes in practice and could not find it. Not in your annual report, not on your site, not in any filing. No measure of the median hours from registration to the intake forms coming back complete, none for the hours to a confirmed appointment, and none for the share of registrations that never reach one.

That last one is the number we would want first. A person who registers and is never booked leaves no trace in a schedule, so nothing surfaces them unless something is counting. For scale, 9,500 dollars is roughly a fifth of what one Patient Financial Services Representative II costs in a year at the floor of your posted range on a 2,080 hour year.

Three measures, none published

Median hours to forms returned, median hours to a confirmed appointment, and the share of registrations that never reach one. We could find no published figure for any of the three.

You have shown what the difference looks like. Your CMO can describe a before and an after in his own words, which is more than anyone outside can do for the front door, and it is a description rather than a measurement.

04

What we would build, and there is no AI in the part we are asking you to buy

A trigger, a send log, a date calculator, and a screen where a person confirms.

A trigger that notices your online registration form has been submitted. A message send with fixed reminders and a log your staff can read. A date calculator that knows your sliding fee rules and has tests. A screen where a staff member confirms what is on a form before it is filed. A kill switch that puts everything back the way it is today. That is phase one, and there is no model anywhere in it.

When a registration arrives, the intake form links go out at once instead of waiting out the published window. Reminders follow on a fixed clock and flag anything that stalls, so staff see who stopped partway, which today nothing counts. The three measurements run from day one, split by channel, because a text and web path can't reach the people your mobile clinic reaches at shelters and a blended number would flatter this.

Phase one is everything except the model box, which stays switched off. The trigger, the reminder ladder and the sliding fee date rules are ours to build, the packet store keeps the patient's own words beside every value, and the review screen is where a person confirms before anything is filed. Nothing here writes to a scheduling system, so your staff book the appointment exactly as they do now. On any failure the patient receives the same links and the same callback they get today.

A working demonstration, built on your own facts. It is a demonstration and it is honest about that, and it does nothing this study did not scope. Open it full screen.

We should tell you what our own scoring said, because it argues against the thing we sell. We scored five candidate builds against your published process, and the version with no model in it came out ahead of the version with one by a wide margin. We did not explain that away. Firing a text when a form arrives is a rules problem, so phase one is rules only. Two model steps sit in a later phase, reading what a patient types into candidate values on a form, and answering the narrow set of administrative questions you already publish. Both sit behind a person confirming every packet, and neither gets funded unless the measured result below clears. The measured wins in your field are real. Across 8,581 clinicians at five academic health systems, independent work found ambient documentation associated with about 13 fewer minutes of record time. OCHIN, the EHR collaborative a community health center belongs to, reports 14 percent less note time a day per active clinician at that member, closer to your setting though it is a collaborative reporting on its own member rather than an outside audit. Neither was measured at a single site health center in Alaska, so applying either to you is an assumption and we name it as one. The clause that belongs beside both is that the same study found time outside work hours did not move significantly. Three things we would not do at any phase. We would not modify or replace your ambient scribe, which is working and is not ours to disturb. We would not do live spoken interpretation, because the review that supports written AI translation concludes in the same paper that human interpreters remain essential for complex, emotionally charged or high stakes clinical communication. We would not build clinical triage, because a false negative on a message about self harm has no ceiling and no data exists to estimate how often it would happen.

We would buy the commodity and build only what is yours. Bought: message delivery, hosting, staff sign-in through whatever your people already use, and human translation of your four published forms once, which then becomes a static file. Built: the trigger, the reminders, the sliding fee date rules with their tests, and the review screen. One thing we would deliberately not buy is a patient engagement platform, because they are sold per provider per seat, and safety net leaders told the California Health Care Foundation plainly that those pricing models do not work for them. Buying one would also leave the integration work undone, which the same research says is what a clinic without data scientists on staff is short of.

The honest prior for any AI proposal, including this one, is that it will not pay. MIT's NANDA report put only about 5 percent of enterprise AI pilots at rapid revenue acceleration. That figure is directional and contested, its denominator is argued over, and it counts pilots that did not move a number rather than technology that does not work, so we use it to frame rather than to bludgeon. It points the same way as the survey below, where even the best scoring category succeeded for about half of respondents. What puts phase one on the other side is that most of it is not AI, it degrades to your current process on any failure, and it needs no write access to a scheduling system you do not fully control.

Your Chief Operations Officer should own the number, with a variance check 90 days after go live against the baseline collected before the send is switched on, split by channel. The pass condition, the same wherever it appears, is that a second phase is fundable only if the median hours from registration to forms returned falls by at least half and no channel gets worse. Below that we would tell you not to spend it.

07

Four ways this is a bad buy, and the first is that the window might not be a lag at all

Four ways this is a bad buy, and we would rather you found them here.

The window might not be a lag at all. Your pages say the intake links arrive within one to two business days and never say why, so if somebody is verifying identity or checking for a duplicate chart in there, an instant send is the wrong build. That is question one in week one, before any code, and if a check exists the build becomes a same day service level with the check kept, at the same fee. The discovery is what you are buying, so that outcome costs you nothing.

The automatic send might move almost nothing. If it does, the bottleneck sits where we can't see it, most likely in appointment supply, which this build never touches and makes no claim about. We would write that up as a recommendation against the second phase. You would keep the send, the tested rules and three numbers, and stop spending on a theory your own data just disproved.

This build structurally can't reach some of your patients. A text and web path is no use to someone without a stable number or a device, which includes people your mobile clinic reaches at shelters. Worse, the completion numbers could improve while exactly those patients are untouched. That is why every measure is reported split by channel, and why the guardrail fails if any channel gets worse.

The evidence here is more modest than the noise suggests. In a survey of 43 health systems, 77 percent named immature tools as a top barrier, and even clinical documentation, the highest scoring category, drew a high degree of success from only 53 percent of respondents. That is why the part we are asking you to buy has no model in it.
