Field study prepared for Calista Corporation

Calista publishes a roster of Shareholders it can't reach, and none of what it costs is published anywhere.

Anchorage and the Yukon-Kuskokwim DeltaJuly 31, 2026

In short

Calista maintains a page called the Shareholder Address Unknown List. It was updated on July 1, 2026, and the page describes the people on it as "Shareholders unable to receive distribution checks, voting materials or newsletters by mail." No count is printed on it. We counted the entries on the page and got 127. That is our count of your page, not a figure you published, and you can repeat it.

On another page Calista tells Shareholders what the mail channel costs them. A distribution sent by direct deposit arrives the same day. Sent by mail it takes three weeks or longer in rural Alaska. Those are Calista's own descriptions, quoted in full later in this study.

We spent this study on that gap. What we would build is a work queue for Shareholder Services that gathers every piece of evidence about one unreachable name onto a single screen, proposes candidate matches using rules a person can read line by line, and then refuses to change anything until a named person confirms it. Confirmed Shareholders, and a cohort you choose from those still on mail, get a one-tap invite to direct deposit and electronic delivery that opens on a phone and works with no signal.

Phase one contains no model at all. The one place a model would genuinely help, reading scanned paper, does not appear until a second phase and ships only if it passes a test agreed beforehand. If that test set does not exist, it does not ship and the rules stand alone.

The honest cost picture is a range with one end that says no. Our conservative case recovers about 32 percent of five-year cost and never pays back. Our middle case pays back around month 44, which is slow for a build this size and we are not going to dress it up. That figure also assumes a quarter of invited Shareholders accept, a rate nobody has measured, and at a tenth it does not pay back at all. What separates them is how large an invitation programme you decide to run and what a mailed item really costs you, and neither is published. That is exactly why we are proposing a two-week look at your records for six to eight thousand, and then a ten-week pilot at $40,500 if it survives that, plus about a thousand dollars of print and postage and your own staff's time on the follow-ups, which sit outside that number and are described below.

What we could not verify matters as much. Calista publishes no case volume, no backlog, no cycle time, and no split between Shareholders paid by mail and by direct deposit. Producing eight such numbers is the first deliverable. Seven land by week eight and the eighth, the acceptance rate, lands at week twenty, and together they are what should decide whether anyone spends more, including on us.

What we are asking for is an hour, not a decision.

01

Calista already publishes the problem, and keeps it current

The strongest evidence in this study is a page Calista maintains itself.

Most companies make you go looking for the operational gap. Calista posts it. The Shareholder Address Unknown List carries the names of owners of the corporation who can't receive their distribution checks, their voting materials, or their newsletter, and it was refreshed on July 1, 2026. That it is public and current is a governance choice we would defend, not a criticism.

What the page does not carry is a number. We counted the entries and got 127. We are telling you how we got it because you can open the page and count it yourself.

Against a base Andrew Guy described to the Senate Committee on Indian Affairs as "more than 39,500 Alaska Native Shareholders, with approximately 20,000 living throughout our ancestral Homelands in the Yukon-Kuskokwim Region," 127 is a small share. It is also the share where the corporation's basic obligation to its owners is not being met.

127

Names on the July 1, 2026 roster. Our count of the page, which publishes no total of its own.

The distributions work next door to this is visibly manual, and Calista describes it that way itself. Calista's live posting for a Shareholder Distributions Coordinator asks that person to "Research and calculate payments owed to deceased Shareholders and identify those that must be voided or stopped then redistributed to rightful heirs," and notes that "Although the standard work week is scheduled at 40 hours, overtime hours may be required to meet objectives and project deadlines."

02

The cost is a three-week wait, and the rest is not published anywhere

We can describe the shape of what this costs. We can't honestly size it, and neither can anyone else from outside.

Calista's own distributions page states the cost, and it is written for Shareholders rather than for a proposal. Direct deposit arrives "The same day as the distribution, but verify with your individual bank." Mail takes "3 weeks or longer, especially in rural Alaska, from the day of distribution." For a Shareholder on the roster there is no arrival date at all, and the ballot and the newsletter do not arrive either.

Same day against three weeks

The gap between the two ways Calista pays a distribution, in Calista's own description of them.

We looked for eight numbers and found none of them published: how many Shareholders can't be reached, how much mail comes back each distribution cycle, the split between mail, direct deposit and electronic delivery, how long a returned mailing waits before anyone works it, how many reconnections are confirmed rather than merely matched, how many staff minutes a confirmation and an enrollment follow-up each take, what a printed and mailed item actually costs you, and what share of invited Shareholders accept. Producing those eight is the first deliverable of the pilot, seven by week eight and the acceptance rate at week twenty, and it is why the pilot is worth buying on its own.

03

What changes is that a name stops waiting for a relative to notice it

Walk one returned envelope through the process as it runs today, and then as it would run.

The outcome we would aim at is narrow on purpose. Reconnect the Shareholders Calista can't currently reach, and move more Shareholders onto same-day direct deposit and electronic delivery, so fewer owners wait three weeks or longer for money that is already theirs.

Deciding that two records are the same person is the one step we would not automate at any confidence level, because a wrong match removes the name from the roster while the Shareholder still receives nothing, and the only person who would notice is the one you already can't reach.

A match score is stored against a candidate and never against a Shareholder record, and the software that proposes matches has no permission to record a confirmation at all. A record moves only when a person acts on evidence they can see.

04

Most of what we would build is not AI

A work queue, matching rules a person can read, an evidence panel, an audit trail, and an enrollment invite that survives a satellite connection.

A list of the people you can't reach. A table of name and village spellings your own staff write. A matcher that proposes who each person might be. A screen showing the evidence. A confirm button only a person can press. An invite that opens on a phone with no signal. A record of who decided what, and when.

The queue takes the roster and returned mail as its work list. The matcher compares each unreachable name against your records using rules a person can read, chiefly a table of Yukon-Kuskokwim name and village spellings your own staff write and Calista owns outright. Every candidate lands in a state called matched but unconfirmed and stays there. A person in Shareholder Services opens the evidence, confirms or rejects with a reason, and only then does a record change. The enrollment invite then goes to two groups, the Shareholders you reconnect and a cohort you choose from those currently receiving by mail. The AIRRAQ network covers 13 Western Alaska communities, seven more villages are due by the end of 2027, and most of the rest run on satellite, so an invite that needs a live connection is an invite much of the region can't accept, which is why it stores answers on the phone and sends them when signal returns.

Read left to right. Calista's records and a variant table your staff write go in. The match finder proposes candidates and decides nothing. Everything waits in the queue until a person in Shareholder Services confirms it against the evidence. Only then does a record change, signed with who did it. Nothing on this page uses a model.

A working demonstration, built on your own facts. It is a demonstration and it is honest about that, and it does nothing this study did not scope. Open it full screen.

Phase one contains no model at all. Chain four judgments together, each right nine times in ten, and the arithmetic lands at about two thirds right end to end, which across a roster this size is dozens of wrong records, wrong silently. With no model in the path, reliability is something you verify by testing rather than estimate.

One place a model would genuinely earn its keep is reading scanned and handwritten records that rules can't read. That is phase two, and it is gated. If roughly 100 past resolutions do not exist in a form we can test against, or the step does not clear an accuracy bar written down before we run it, it does not ship and the rules stand alone.

You already answered the hardest question here yourselves, when you posted a Shareholder Services receptionist where "Ability to speak Yupik strongly preferred." We would not sell anything that quietly displaces that person, and we would not build anything that translates Yugtun. Two separate things support that, and they are worth keeping apart. A California contractor, Accent on Languages, produced Yup'ik disaster materials that KYUK translator Julia Jimmie described by saying "They were Yup'ik words all right, but they were all jumbled together, and they didn't make sense." That was human work. Separately, reporting on machine translation of these languages describes a poor record, "often providing inaccurate sentences and even completely invented words." In a region where nearly half the population, some 10,000 people, learn Yugtun before English, neither of those is a risk worth taking with your own language.

We would buy the hosting, the sign-in, the postal address lookup and the message delivery, all inside your own cloud account. We would build the queue, because the case-management tools we have used resolve a record once a score crosses a threshold, and the first thing we would do is check whether yours can be made to refuse. That behaviour is what would drop a Shareholder off the roster while she still receives nothing.

You could also build much of this yourselves. Calista has a Chief Information Officer, and a Full Stack Developer role open in Huntsville. If your answer on the call is that your own team should build the rest, that is a good outcome and we would scope it that way.

The conservative case does not pay back, and that is the argument for a small pilot

Three scenarios, one of which says no. What separates them is how many Shareholders you are willing to invite and what your own mail actually costs, and neither of those is published.

Mail handling avoided is the standing cohort times touches times cost per item times the share that disappears, and the standing cohort is the yearly acceptances accumulated over the horizon. Reconnection carrying cost applies that same share to $60, $75 and $90 a year per unresolved record, covering returned postage, void and reissue banking fees and the unclaimed-property administration on a record nobody can deliver to, and it contains no staff time. Reissue cost applies that share to 1.5, 2.0 and 2.5 percent of the checks that would otherwise have been mailed to that cohort, at $55, $65 and $75 an event. The Shareholders you reconnect also join the electronic cohort, so a small part of those last two lines overlaps. On the same average-standing basis the rest of the table uses, that is roughly two to three percent of the annual benefit depending on the column, and we have not netted it out. The reconnection line also assumes the queue extends past the published roster to all returned mail, which is a Next-lane item and not in the ten-week price. The 127 names on the roster alone support roughly three and a half years at the conservative rate and under two at the aggressive one, and how much returned mail arrives after that is one of the numbers the pilot measures. The look is quoted at six to eight thousand and the model uses the top of that range. The invitation programme is now costed rather than assumed away, and its volume scales with the column, because an invitation is itself a printed and mailed item. At the gate's rate of a quarter accepting, 700 acceptances needs 2,800 invitations a year, 1,600 needs 6,400, and 3,000 needs 12,000. The Aggressive column therefore requires more than four times the volume the gate paragraph describes, and 3,000 acceptances could not come from 2,800 invitations at all. Against the more than 39,500 Shareholders quoted earlier, that column means inviting close to a third of everyone you have, every year for five years. It is printed as a ceiling. We would not plan on it and we did not use it for the payback figure. Those invitation costs sit in run cost above. What is still NOT costed anywhere is the staff time to run that programme, and it is large enough to move the answer. At the middle column's 1,600 acceptances a year, even ten minutes of follow-up each is roughly 270 hours, about seven weeks of one person's time, against a $42,593 annual benefit, and it would move it against us. The pilot's own 500 invitations, roughly $600 to $1,050 of print and postage, sit outside the $40,500 as well. Assumptions we chose, rather than facts about Calista: the acceptance count, the touches, the two distributions a year, the item cost, the share that disappears, the reconnection rate, the $60 to $90 a year carrying cost per unresolved record, the 1.5 to 2.5 percent reissue rate, the $55 to $75 per reissue event, and the quarter of invitations that accept. That is the complete list of numbers we picked. The last one is the sharpest. At a tenth accepting rather than a quarter the conservative column needs 7,000 invitations a year and the middle column needs 16,000, and on those numbers the middle case recovers 78 percent of its cost and never pays back either. Note what 16,000 means. It is more than the 12,000 the Aggressive column needs, which is already close to a third of every Shareholder you have, so at a tenth accepting the middle case is not merely slower, it needs a mailing programme larger than the ceiling we just said we would not plan on. The pilot's acceptance leg exists to test that rate before anyone spends against it. No hours-times-rate line appears anywhere.

A two-week look, then ten weeks of work against a stop rule that closes at week twenty

The conservative column is the floor below which we tell you to stop, and the middle column is the shape of worth building. That column pays back at month 44, which is slow. Most software of this cost is judged against twelve to twenty-four months, and a payback past two years is normally a reason to ask whether this is the right solution at all. It is exactly why the decision belongs at week twenty on measured numbers rather than on this model, because even the model's good case is slow.

The first two weeks are the look, and your own staff work your own records on your own machines while we observe and time it, so nothing leaves the building. In the pilot that follows, everything we touch before records clearance runs on the public roster and synthetic records, so one record is carried the whole way before any real record moves to us.

07

The evidence to reconnect these people may not be in your records

Three things, and we would rather raise them than have you find them.

The riskiest assumption is that evidence to reconnect these Shareholders already exists inside Calista's own records. If it does not, the matcher has nothing to work with and the right answer is an outreach campaign, not a work queue. We would retire that first, with the two-week look described above, six to eight thousand dollars, run in your office on your machines with no data leaving the building. Take forty names from the roster, have your own staff work them while we time it, and write the decision rule down beforehand. If fewer than 16 of the 40 have any internal evidence, we say so in writing and the pilot does not proceed.

The second is that our headline metric could lie to us. A shrinking roster looks like success even when a record was matched to the wrong person, so confirmed reconnections are counted separately, every resolution is audited rather than sampled, and the reconnection line in the business case counts only confirmed reconnections, never matches.

The third is that you may build this yourselves, and we have said so above rather than hoping it does not come up. The pilot is shaped so everything of lasting value belongs to you whether we build phase two or not. Landing exactly on the conservative column is not a reason to build either, because that column loses money every year. It is the floor below which we stop.
