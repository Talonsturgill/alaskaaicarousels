In short

We spent a day on Tyonek from the outside, using only public records, and this is what we would tell you if you had hired us.

The thing worth your attention is a single sentence in a solicitation you already know. Air Force RFP FA8773-25-R-0002 says affiliate past performance will be considered "provided that sufficient documentation is included in the proposal", then closes with the part that costs money: if that information is not included, the affiliate companies "shall not be evaluated or considered". The penalty is total, and the provision puts no duty on anyone to tell you it happened.

That sentence sits on an unusual structure. USASpending lists 22 child registrations under your parent UEI, with near duplicate names registered separately by state, including four whose names begin TFAB Ground Systems and three registered as Tyonek Engineering & Agile Manufacturing. Four of your companies carry SBA 8(a) certification. We can't see how your executed agreements are held from outside, which is part of what the half day checks.

Nothing we could verify shows Tyonek has ever failed to document an affiliate. GAO dismissed Tyonek Technical Innovations' protest in B-424364 on timing, not on merit, partly because the solicitation's language was, in GAO's words, unambiguously clear. That reads as close attention rather than as a slip. Our argument is about the size and the one way nature of the exposure, never about a failure we can't see.

So the recommendation is smaller than you would expect. Buy half a day, not a build. Our fee is $4,500. With your people's time in it, the half day runs to about $7,000. We model the decision itself as worth $19,500 to $42,900 against that, between $2.75 and $7.31 back for every dollar, and the table shows both ends, though part of that value is simply your not spending money with us. Let us measure four numbers off proposals you have already submitted. They are listed further down, and the one that matters most is how many of your bids even carry this provision.

The build, if the numbers ever justify it, is an indexed set of your executed agreements, a register of your 22 entities keyed on their registration numbers, and a rule that checks one against the other before you send. It never pays back in cash in any case we modelled, and it only approaches it at 22 qualifying bids a year, a number we chose and not your registration count.

We have written a kill condition into this study, before any money moves. If all three of these hold, completeness already above about 90 percent, assembly under a day a bid, and fewer than about eight qualifying bids a year, we will tell you in writing not to build it.

01

One sentence in an Air Force solicitation you already bid decides whether an affiliate's record counts

The provision is short, we have verified it in one of your solicitations, and it carries no duty to tell you when it bites.

Air Force RFP FA8773-25-R-0002, issued 19 May 2025 as an 8(a) set aside, spells out how affiliate past performance gets treated. Affiliate, sister, teaming and joint venture arrangements are considered when sufficient documentation rides along in the proposal, and the prime must demonstrate the affiliate will perform significant and critical aspects of the contract. Then the sentence that carries the risk: "If the required information is not included in the proposal, the proposed affiliate companies, sister companies, joint venture companies, etc. shall not be evaluated or considered."

Read that as an operations problem rather than a legal one. An affiliate whose paperwork is short does not get marked down. It stops existing for that evaluation. The work was done, the rating is on file, and none of it counts.

02

A struck affiliate is a total loss, and nothing obliges anyone to tell you it happened

Most process problems cost you a percentage. This one costs you the whole item.

When an affiliate is struck for missing documentation, the past performance you meant to lean on is gone from that evaluation and the evaluator sees a thinner company than the one that exists.

The provision imposes no duty on the agency to tell you. Whether it can be cured depends on whether the agency opens discussions, which you can't count on.

04

Most of what we would build is a filing system and a rule, and the rule is where we refuse to put a model

Most of the value here is a filing system and a rule.

Four pieces. A collected set of your executed affiliation, teaming and joint venture agreements, gathered from wherever they live now and indexed. A register of your 22 entities keyed on their registration numbers. A rule that compares what a solicitation demands against what is actually on file. Finally, a one page gap list a named person signs before the proposal goes out.

On a given bid, the rule takes the affiliates you intend to cite and reports, for each one and each required document, whether it is present, missing, expired or ambiguous. Anything not verified against a page in a document reports as missing.

Two places use a model, and neither can change that verdict. The first reads a scanned agreement as it is filed and proposes what it is, who signed it and when it expires, refusing to answer without pointing at the page. A person confirms that once, at filing, and the confirmed fact is what the rule reads later. The second drafts the affiliate narrative from facts a person has already approved.

A third use is possible, a second reading of the solicitation to flag where it disagrees with yours. We left it out, because reading the provision takes a capture manager about ten minutes, which we are assuming rather than measuring, and a second read does not earn its keep at your bid volume.

Read left to right. Two things meet at the check in the middle, your filed agreements along the top and the bid you are about to send. The check is a rule, not a model. The reading step is confirmed by a person before anything it produced can be used, and the drafting step only runs after a person has signed. Neither model can move a go or no go.

A working demonstration, built on your own facts. It is a demonstration and it is honest about that, and it does nothing this study did not scope. Open it full screen.

The check is a list compared against a list, not a prediction. It gives the same answer every time and can be tested exhaustively, which is why we would not put a model near it. Entity matching runs on registration numbers only, never on a name.

There are three places we would refuse to go. Anything touching a maintenance sign off or return to service, because 14 CFR 145.213(d) reserves that to a certificated mechanic or repairman. Anything drawing-derived, including AS9102 first article content, if it is export controlled, because a hosted model has to decrypt your data to read it and we read 22 CFR 120.54 as putting that outside the carve out. Your government property records are the third, because they are rule governed and structured, and you posted an analyst in July to own them.

We would also not recommend predictive maintenance to you. GAO reports the services "generally lack metrics to evaluate the results of predictive maintenance". The DoD Inspector General found the department had not fully implemented it on any existing weapon system. Your capability page mentions no AI at all, and on this particular thing that is the right answer, not a gap.

Buy the storage, the identity, the text recognition for scanned pages, and the model itself. Never train one. What is worth building is narrow: the register keyed on your registration numbers, the rule set and the cases that prove it, and a check inside that first reading step that keeps export marked documents away from any outside service. If you already run a system holding these agreements, extending it beats standing up anything new.

07

Four things could make us wrong, and the first is that you may not have this problem

Four things, and the first is the one that would cost you the most to discover late.

The first is that this may not be a problem you have. Your completeness rate might already be at or near 100 percent, in which case the build is worth far less than we modelled and possibly nothing. That is exactly why the ask is half a day of measurement with a kill condition attached.

The second is that our confidence is genuinely moderate. We put the chance that this is a live gap at your company at a bit under a coin flip, 0.4 to 0.5, and the reason cuts against us: GAO dismissed Tyonek Technical Innovations' protest partly because the provision's language was unambiguously clear, which is a point in favour of a capture team complying with it unaided. That is a different question from the one priced further down, where we ask how often the measurement actually changes what you decide and put that at 1 in 4 to just over half.

The third is that the hardest part is not the clever part. Finding executed agreements across a company operating in 16 states can take far longer than anyone estimates, and some may not be findable at all. We priced that work inside the scope rather than calling it your homework.

The fourth is ownership. Your headquarters is in Anchorage while your manufacturing sits in Madison, Huntsville and Warner Robins and your repair stations in Kiln and Huntsville, so the people who would use this are spread across several states from the people who would fund it. Your about page names Joe Webb as Vice President, Business Development, and we have no way to see from outside whether federal capture sits with him. A build with nobody clearly owning it is the most reliable way for this kind of project to quietly stop, so the first conversation should settle who owns the completeness number. If nobody does, we would say so and recommend against starting.
