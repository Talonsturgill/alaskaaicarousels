Your storage clock starts when you tell a consignee in writing, so that notice is a billing document.

Anchorage and seven bush stationsAugust 3, 2026

In short

Your freight rules page says storage fees may be applied for shipments held more than three days after written notification of arrival at the destination point, at $.75 per 100 pounds or part thereof, per day. Read that closely. The clock does not start when the freight lands. It starts when the consignee is told, in writing, that it landed. That sentence turns an arrival notice into a billing document at every station you run, and it means a fee you already publish is only as solid as the record of the notice behind it.

The raw material already exists. Your Bethel Lead Cargo Agent posting says an on hand reconciliation is completed daily, so once a day that station knows exactly what is sitting there. What we can't see from outside is what happens next, whether every arriving airbill produces a written notice, how fast, and where those notices are kept.

So we would build a small desk that turns that daily count into a written, timestamped notice per consignee, drafted automatically and released by the agent who handled the freight that day. Nothing a shipper reads would be written by a model. Finding what newly arrived is a comparison against yesterday, the storage date is arithmetic on the rule you publish, the notice is a fixed template. One place a model might appear, reading the daily sheet, and it gets deleted if that sheet is already a file.

Here is the part most proposals leave out. We priced that six week build and its conservative case did not clear, so we are not asking you to buy it yet. What we recommend buying first is two weeks of measurement at Bethel, and the deliverable is the decision itself. Our fee for that is about $7,200 to $8,300 including contingency, plus roughly twenty hours of your own people's time, which we have not priced.

Be careful with the return we put on those two weeks. It rests on one thing, the rework you avoid by not committing blind, and that is worth something only if a second station is genuinely on the table. If Bethel is the whole ambition, the quantified return is zero and what you are buying is a decision record and two baselines you do not have today.

We could not verify your shipment volumes, call counts or contact data, so every figure here is a modeled illustration and labeled as one.

Before any of that, do the free thing. Five policies missing from your rules page are worth more per hour than anything we would charge you for.

01

Your own tariff makes the arrival notice a billing document

The most interesting sentence on your website is in the freight rules.

Storage fees only apply to shipments held more than three days after written notification of arrival, not more than three days after arrival. Every day of storage you ever charge traces back to a piece of writing and the date it went out.

That makes the notice unusual. It is a service to the consignee, a way to get freight off your floor, and the basis for a fee, all in one document.

Your Nome Traffic Coordinator posting names the outbound half, notifying village agents and customers of flight schedules, ETAs and load onboard. The posting does not say how. What it does say is that a person does it, shipment by shipment.

04

Almost none of this needs a model, and the one that might gets deleted if your sheet is a file

A small desk between the count you already do and the notice you already owe.

The moving pieces, in plain nouns. A folder where the daily count lands. A comparison against yesterday. A list of consignees and how to reach them. A date calculator holding your three day rule. A fixed notice template. One review screen with a release button. A stored log of every notice and the time it went. Email and text delivery. That is the whole thing.

Each day the desk takes the on hand list, compares it against yesterday to find what newly arrived, matches each line to a consignee, and computes the date fees would begin under your published rule. It drafts one notice per consignee carrying the shipment reference, the arrival time and that date, with your storage rule quoted as written. A Bethel agent opens one screen, sees only the new lines and anything uncertain, and releases the batch or holds a line.

If a notice goes out wrong, one button retracts and corrects it, and that shipment is permanently marked so no storage fee can be raised on it.

Two ways in, and we expect only one gets built. Nothing reaches a consignee until a Bethel agent releases the batch.

A working demonstration, built on your own facts. It is a demonstration and it is honest about that, and it does nothing this study did not scope. Open it full screen.

Finding what newly arrived is a comparison between two lists. The storage date is arithmetic on a rule you publish. Matching a consignee is name matching with a threshold, and anything below it goes to a person, because a computer's best guess about who owns a shipment is exactly the error that costs money. The notice is a fixed template. All of those can be tested line by line in a way no model can.

One place a model might appear, reading the daily sheet, and only if it is paper. If it comes out of Takeflite as a file, that piece is deleted and the build gets cheaper and more reliable at once.

We declined two things. No voice agent on your station phone. Every question it would field is a lookup or a sum ordinary software does the same way every time and that you can test exhaustively, and the likely ones are all money statements, a rate quote carrying a stack of interacting modifiers, an arrival answer that starts your storage clock, a refund promise against your published ten business day window. In 2024 a tribunal held that a carrier is responsible for what its own website tells a customer. We are also staying out of dispatch and weather, where the cost of an error is a human life.

Ask before you buy anything, including from us. Takeflite already runs reservations, freight, dispatch and scheduling in this market, so it may already send arrival notification, and Northern Air Cargo, a sibling inside Saltchuk Aviation, already ships a self serve rate estimator. If Takeflite already sends arrival notification, stop there. Nothing below gets funded and we bill nothing.

Otherwise we would buy the commodity and build only what encodes your rules. The notice log is the actual product, because it is the record your storage clause depends on. We would ask for no Takeflite credentials.

07

Four things could sink this, and consignees you can't reach in writing can sink it outright

The first one has no fallback.

Your consignees may not be reachable in writing. If most are reachable only by a call to a village agent or a sheet at the counter, the sending half of this is aimed at a channel that does not exist, and phase one is capturing contact details at the counter, a different project at a different price. That is why we want to count 200 airbills before building anything, and if it comes back thin we will say so and reshape or recommend stopping.

The review could quietly turn into rubber stamping. If a day at Bethel runs anything like the one in the demonstration below, thirteen lines with six new, the batch is small enough to approve without reading by week three. So we count how often the agent changes something before releasing, and watch it in both directions. Too many changes means our comparison is wrong. A week with none means the reading stopped.

A wrong notice costs more than a wrong email. It starts a fee clock your customer did not earn, on a record you created, so everything is tuned toward holding rather than sending.

Somebody may already sell you this. That is why the Takeflite ticket asks whether they already send arrival notification, and why a yes stops the whole thing. That question costs you nothing and might cost us the engagement.
